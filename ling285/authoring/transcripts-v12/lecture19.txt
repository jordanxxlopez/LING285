9|**An LLM learns by guessing a hidden word and then adjusting its weights.** In the sentence “The chef cracks the,” the last word is hidden. The model reads the earlier words and has to predict the word that comes next. >> **Each earlier word gives a clue about the hidden word.** “The,” “chef,” “cracks” and “the” are each linked to the empty slot. These links are **interdependencies**, which are relationships in which one part of a sequence helps predict another part. A chef who cracks something makes a food word likely. || **The model does not know the answer, so it spreads its bets.** A bet is the probability the model gives to one possible word. The largest bet is on “egg,” a smaller bet is on “nut,” and the smallest is on “code.” All three could follow, but the earlier words make “egg” the most likely. >> **Learning is nudging the weights so that these guesses get better.** A nudge is a small change to a weight. After the true word is revealed, the weights change a little, so the right word gets a bigger bet the next time. >> The model’s three bets are unequal. The bet on “egg” is the largest, the bet on “nut” is smaller, and the bet on “code” is the smallest. || **The same idea holds when the signal contains more than words.** The signal is the sequence the model reads. It can hold **words, sounds and images** together, and the model still guesses a hidden piece from the other pieces. The content of the signal changes, while the way the model learns stays the same. >> All four earlier words are linked to the hidden slot, so a clue can come from the start of the sentence as well as from the word right before the slot.
10|**An LLM can only read a row of vectors, and not all information arrives in that form.** Text, speech and images reach the model in three different forms. Speech and images have to be converted before the model can use them. >> **Text is already a row of pieces.** The sentence “Now crack the egg” separates into the pieces “Now,” “crack,” “the” and “egg.” Each piece can become one vector, so text is easy to give to the model. || **Speech is air pressure changing in time.** The pressure goes up and down continuously, and nothing in the sound marks where one word stops and the next one starts. There are no words in the sound itself. >> **An image is a grid of light.** Each position in the grid has its own brightness and colour. The image of an egg in a pan contains no words either, and its pieces are not arranged in a row. >> The speech example is a wave of air pressure with no gaps that mark words, and the image example is an egg in a pan. || **The first job is a conversion.** All three forms have to become rows of vectors, because a row of vectors is all the LLM can read. Once speech and images are rows too, one model can read them together with text. >> The task that follows is turning all three forms into rows of vectors.
11|**Speech is converted by slicing time into short frames.** A **frame** is one short slice of the sound. The speech is cut at regular points in time, and each slice is about 20 milliseconds long. >> **The slices go through a speech encoder.** A **speech encoder** is a neural network that was trained on a large amount of audio. It receives the slices of sound and turns each one into a vector. || **One vector comes out for each slice.** Different slices contain different sounds, so their vectors hold different numbers. The different shades in the columns under the encoder stand for those different numbers. >> **One second of speech becomes 50 vectors.** Each slice lasts about 20 milliseconds, and 50 slices of that length fill one second. The vectors stay in time order, so the earliest slice comes first in the row. >> The vector for a slice is a column of numbers, and a slice with a different sound gets different numbers. || **Speech is now a row, just like a sentence.** A sentence is a row of word vectors, and the sliced speech is a row of frame vectors. The LLM reads both the same way, one vector after another. >> Time order matters because the model reads the row from the first vector to the last, the same way it reads the words of a sentence.
12|**An image is converted by cutting it into patches.** A **patch** is one small square of the image. The image of an egg in a pan is cut by a grid into 16 patches, 4 across and 4 down. >> **The patches are laid out in a row, in reading order.** Reading order means left to right along the top, then left to right along the next line down, the way a page is read. The image is now a sequence with a fixed order, like a sentence. || **The patches go through a vision encoder.** A **vision encoder** turns each patch into a vector, in the same way the speech encoder turns each slice of sound into a vector. >> **One vector comes out for each patch.** The 16 patches give 16 vectors, and the vectors keep the order of the patches. The image has become a row that the LLM can read. >> The first patch in the row is the top left square of the image, and the last patch is the bottom right square. || **Real systems use much smaller patches.** The 4 by 4 grid keeps the example simple. Real systems use patches of 14 to 16 pixels, so one image gives hundreds of vectors. >> Smaller patches mean more patches for the same image, which is why real systems get hundreds of vectors where the example gets 16.
13|**Image patches, speech frames and words are placed in one row.** The image patches come first, the speech frames come next, and the words come last. The words are “Now,” “crack” and “the,” and the last word is hidden. >> **The earlier words still give clues.** “Now,” “crack” and “the” each help the model guess the hidden word. These are the same dependencies between words that a model uses when its row holds only text. || **Clues now also come from the other senses.** A patch that contains an egg helps predict the hidden word, and so does the sound of cracking. A link between two different kinds of signal is a **cross-modal dependency**. >> **The game is exactly the same as before.** The model guesses the hidden piece from the pieces it can read. It then learns from how good the guess was. >> The example row has four image patches, four speech frames and the three words, followed by the hidden slot. || **The source of the clues is what changes.** With text alone, every clue is a word. In the combined row, a clue can also be an image patch or a speech frame, so the model has more information for the same guess. >> A cross-modal dependency works in the same direction as a dependency between words, from a piece the model can read to the piece it has to guess.
14|**One event can be given to the model in three forms.** The example is “three cute puppies.” It appears as an image of three puppies, as the phrase spoken aloud, and as the three written words. >> **The image is cut into 18 patches.** The grid has 3 rows of 6 patches. As with the egg, each patch is one piece of the image. || **The spoken phrase is a spectrogram.** A **spectrogram** displays sound with time running from left to right. A higher position means a higher frequency, and a darker area means more energy. >> **Each word occupies its own stretch of time.** “Three” comes first, “cute” comes second and “puppies” comes last. Each thin column of the spectrogram is one frame. The speech was made with a speech synthesizer, an MBROLA diphone voice. >> The three puppies have different colours, and the third puppy is dark. || **The image, the sound and the words are three views of one event.** In training, all three go into one row. The model then plays the same game on that row, in which one piece is hidden and the model guesses it. >> The words are marked along the time direction of the spectrogram, so each word can be matched to the frames that hold its sound.
15|**In the first training example, the hidden piece is a word.** The row holds the image first, then the sound, then the words. The words are “three” and “cute,” and the last word is hidden. >> **With the words alone, many endings fit.** “Kittens,” “puppies” and “babies” can all follow “three cute.” The model’s bets are spread out across the three words, and no word is far ahead. || **The image and the sound add clues.** The image contains puppies, and the sound contains the spoken word “puppies.” With these clues the bet piles up on “puppies,” and “kittens” and “babies” get very small bets. >> **The true word is then revealed, and the model is graded.** The grade depends on how much the model bet on the true word. The true word is “puppies,” and the model bet 0.85 on it. >> With the words alone, “kittens” is slightly ahead of “puppies” and “babies.” With the image and the sound, “puppies” takes almost the whole bet. || **A large bet on the truth gives a small nudge.** The model bet a lot on the true word, so the error is small. Because the error is small, the weights move only a little. >> A smaller bet on the true word would mean a larger error, and a larger error moves the weights more.
16|**In the second training example, the hidden piece is a frame of sound.** The row is reordered so the image comes first, then the words, then the sound. This is like teaching the model to speak a sentence it was given. The sound is predicted frame by frame from left to right, and the frames to the right have not been heard yet. >> **The first clue comes from the earlier frames.** The speaker’s voice and the **formants** (frequency regions with strong energy) change smoothly. Because they change smoothly, the last few frames say a lot about the next frame. || **The words say which sound should come next.** Here the next sound is the vowel in “pup.” The image contains puppies, so it backs up the words. The image is a weaker clue in this example. >> **A frame is a column of numbers.** Each number is how much energy there is in one frequency band, and 16 bands are displayed. The model’s guess is close to the true frame but not exact. The error is how far apart each number in the guess is from the matching number in the truth. >> The guess and the truth are each a column of 16 values, and the error is largest in the bands where the two columns differ most. || **That error nudges the weights.** The weights change so that the next guess for a frame is closer. Some systems predict a code for each frame instead of the raw numbers. >> Predicting from left to right means the model can use only the frames it has already heard, never the frames that come after the hidden one.
17|**In the third training example, the hidden piece is a patch of the image.** The row is reordered again so the words come first, then the sound, then the image. This is like teaching the model to draw what it was told. The patches are predicted in reading order, and the patches that are not drawn yet are faded. >> **The earlier patches give the first clue.** Above the hidden patch is background. To its left are two puppies, so the model has already seen what a puppy in this image looks like. || **The words help in a different way.** “Three” says a third puppy must appear, and only two have been drawn. “Puppies” says to expect dog ears. The sound carries the same words, so it is a backup for the text. >> **The guess is a blurry puppy ear in the average colour of the first two puppies.** The true patch belongs to a dark puppy. The biggest error is therefore the colour, because nothing in the words, the sound or the earlier patches said the third puppy would be dark. >> The hidden patch is the place where the head of the third puppy belongs. || **That error nudges the weights.** Only systems that also produce images are trained this way. Systems that only read images usually skip this step. >> The guess still has the right content, a puppy ear, because the words and the earlier patches gave that. Only the colour had no clue.
18|**Any piece of the row can be the hidden one.** The hidden piece can be a word, a sound frame or an image patch. In each case, the words, the sound and the image each give a clue. >> **For a hidden word, all three senses point to “puppies.”** The words “three cute” call for a plural noun. The sound helps because the word was just spoken. The image helps because it contains puppies. || **For a hidden frame, the sound itself says the most about what comes next.** The voice and the formants carry on from the earlier frames. The words say which sound comes next, and the image backs up which word is being said. >> **For a hidden patch, the image itself says how the patch should continue.** The fur and the background carry on from the earlier patches. The word “three” says one more puppy is coming, and the sound gives the same words in heard form. >> The three same-sense clues are the plural noun that “three cute” calls for, the voice and formants that carry on, and the fur and background that carry on. || **Clues from the same sense and clues from other senses do different jobs.** A clue from the same sense mostly says how the signal continues, as with grammar, smooth formants, and fur and background. Clues from the other senses mostly say what the thing is, which here is puppies, three of them. Training on all three senses together helps because each sense fills gaps the others leave. >> The other six clues come from a different sense than the hidden piece, and each of them says something about what is being described.
19|**For the model to speak, sound is compressed into discrete codes.** A code is a number that stands for a short piece of sound, such as 417, 82 or 903. Each code works like a new word added to the model’s vocabulary. >> **The model predicts the next code exactly as it predicts the next word.** The earlier codes give clues about the hidden code, so producing speech is the same guessing game again. || **A decoder turns the codes back into sound.** The **decoder** takes the row of predicted codes and produces a sound wave that can be heard. >> **Three things are new.** The first is a shared currency, in which everything becomes vectors in one row. The second is data where the senses appear together. The third is a schedule that protects what was already learned. >> In the example, the codes 417, 82 and 903 are already in the row, and the fourth code is the one being predicted. || **The learning rule stays the same.** The model still finds the dependencies in the signal. The signal has become wider, because it now holds words, sounds and images. >> Because the codes are treated as vocabulary items, the model uses no separate method for speech. It predicts the next item for words and for sound in the same way.
