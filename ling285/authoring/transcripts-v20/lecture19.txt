2|**A single neuron takes a list of numbers and produces one number.** It multiplies the inputs by their weights and adds the products, which is an inner product. A neural network combines many such calculations, so one vector produces a decision vector with a response for each possible answer. >> The example network has layers of four, three, four and five units linked by three sets of weights. || **An LLM takes many vectors and predicts what comes next in the sequence.** Fifteen vectors pass through five layers, and the output is the sixteenth vector, which gives a probability distribution over the next token. >> The layers progressively change the representations, so the prediction uses the processed sequence rather than a single original input vector. || **An agentic system is a graph of LLMs that communicate with one another.** An Outline model plans, a Write model drafts, a Critic model evaluates, a Revise model changes the draft, and a Final model produces the answer. >> The critic and reviser are connected in a loop, so a revision can return for another evaluation. The agents communicate in English, while the models can also work in other human languages and computer languages. || **Inference uses the weights to process an input, while learning changes the weights.** The common operations are inner products for inference and stochastic gradient descent with dynamic programming for learning. >> Backpropagation organizes the backward calculation used to adjust the weights.
3|**Predicting the next word is difficult, so a language model processes its inputs through many layers.** Each layer changes the vectors into representations that can help make the prediction better, and the following layer takes those representations as its own starting point. >> A representation is the information about an input item carried by its vector. || **Each layer also contains several heads working in parallel.** They are described as different experts because they contribute different information to the processing within that layer. Their contributions are brought together to make the layer’s new representations. >> These heads are parts of the language model, rather than the separate LLMs connected into an agentic system. || **The process happens again at the next layer.** Its heads work alongside one another, their contributions are combined, and the resulting vectors continue onward through the network. >> So heads and layers solve different organizational problems. The heads contribute in parallel within a processing stage, while the layers form successive stages that develop the representations further.
4|**A dynamical system takes numbers, applies a rule, and feeds the result back in.** The oscillator begins with position one and velocity zero, and the rule determines how those values change step by step. >> First position is updated using the old velocity. Velocity is then updated using the new position, so the two quantities take turns rather than being updated independently from the old state. || **Repeating that rule builds the oscillator’s trajectory.** Position follows a cosine-like curve and velocity follows a shifted, minus-sine-like curve. >> The step size determines how far the calculation advances each time. || **Jordan’s recurrent network applies feedback to speech production.** The plan and state units feed the hidden layer, which produces output, and a copy of that output returns to the state units. >> Their self-connections also keep a fading trace of the previous state. The same weights are reused at successive moments, so what the system has just produced influences what it produces next. || **An LLM develops its vectors through successive layers, so depth plays the role of time.** Following one value upward through the five layers gives a changing curve, even though it is not a cosine. >> Each layer has its own weights, whereas the oscillator and Jordan network reuse the same rule at each time step. The shared idea is that a new state is computed from the state produced just before it. || The small stock-return graph traces Small, Medium and Large series over the same period. They fluctuate, rise and fall over the years, giving another example of quantities changing over time.
5|**A language model takes a sequence of vectors and predicts the next item.** A vector is a list of numbers, so the same input format can represent different kinds of material. >> The prediction task stays the same even when the sequence comes from a different source. || **The vectors can represent words in a sentence.** In the cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the blank, the task is to predict the missing next word. >> The prediction uses the sentence so far, including the fact that the cat is the one that sat. || **The vectors can represent pieces of sound or a picture.** A spectrogram is divided into consecutive frames, and the model predicts the next frame of sound. A picture is divided into patches read row by row, and the task is to predict the missing bottom-right patch. >> The input represents an unfolding sound in one case and an ordered set of picture pieces in the other. || **The vectors can also represent amino acids in a protein.** The insulin B-chain example gives a sequence of amino acids and leaves the following residue to be predicted. >> The amino acid at the next position is Y. || **Words, sounds, picture patches and amino acids reach the model as sequences of vectors.** The model uses the preceding vectors to predict the following one, even though the original materials are different.
6|English allows only **certain sequences of sounds** in its words, and knowing which sequences are allowed is called **phonotactic knowledge**. **A simple syllable in English can have a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, mat, sit, cup, dog and fish. >> The pattern is about sounds, not letters, so the silent e in shave, kite and rope does not count, and two letters can spell one consonant sound, as in ship, chin and bath. || Syllables can also have **clusters**, groups of consonants together. A cluster before the vowel is an **onset**, as in split, flat and black, and a cluster after the vowel is a **coda**, as in desk, help and lamp. >> Each real word has an impossible twin marked with a star, and many twins use the same letters in the other order, such as flat and lfat, help and hepl, and lamp and lapm, so order matters. >> **The order mirrors around the vowel.** In flat the l sits right next to the vowel with the f farther out, and in help the l again sits next to the vowel, now on the other side, with the p farther out. Each impossible twin puts the wrong consonant against the vowel. || **A long word is a chain of syllables**, such as ba-na-na, At-lan-tic and ath-lete. The sounds t and l are fine across a syllable break, but tl cannot start a syllable, which is why Atlantic is a word but tlantic is impossible. >> The sound ng can end a syllable, as in ring, but it can never start one. >> So whether a sequence of sounds is allowed depends on where the syllable breaks fall.
7|**Phonotactic knowledge can be revealed through GPT learning.** Each item is the start of an English word, and the task is to guess the next letter. People agree on their guesses quickly, and how much they agree changes from item to item. || **After a vowel, almost anything can follow.** After ra, likely letters include t, n, r and l, and many others are possible too, because once a vowel has come the onset is over. >> **At the start of a word, the choices narrow sharply** to exactly what an English onset allows. After sp the next letter is l, r or a vowel and never n, and after k it is a vowel or n, as in knee. >> After b and f it is r, l or a vowel, after sh it is a vowel or r, as in shrimp, and after skr or m at the start of a word only a vowel can come. >> After bram, the letter b is almost forced, as in bramble, though a new syllable could also start there. || **Guessing the next letter is predicting the sixteenth item**, the same next-token game an LLM plays. A GPT trained only on this game learns English sounds and spelling, because its predictions can only get good by matching the patterns of English syllables. >> Its prediction is a probability distribution over the next letter, and the shape of that distribution carries the knowledge. >> After ra the distribution is broad, spread over many letters, while after skr it is sharply peaked on a few vowels, because English allows nothing else there. >> The more the letters so far restrict what can come next, the more the probability piles onto a few letters.

9|**Here is a sentence the model is reading, and its last word is hidden.** The sentence is “The chef cracks the,” and the model has to say what comes after it. >> Each earlier word gives a clue about the hidden word. These are the **interdependencies**, the relationships in which one part of a sequence helps predict another part. || **The model does not know the answer, so it spreads its bets.** It puts the most on “egg,” less on “nut” and the least on “code.” >> A bet is the probability the model gives to a word, and all three words are possible after “The chef cracks the.” || **Learning just means nudging the weights.** After the true word is revealed, the weights move a little, so that next time the right word gets a bigger bet. >> Nothing about this changes when the signal holds sounds and images too. The game is the same, and only the signal is different.
10|**Text is the easy case, because it already comes as a row of pieces.** “Now crack the egg” is four pieces, and each piece can become a vector. >> That row of vectors is exactly what the LLM reads. || **Speech is different, because it is air pressure going up and down in time.** There are no words in it, only a pressure that keeps changing. >> An image is a grid of light, and again there are no words in it. || **So the first job is a conversion.** Everything has to become a row of vectors, because that is all the LLM can read. >> The conversion has to work for all three, for text, for speech and for images.
11|**We take a small piece of speech and cut it into short slices.** Each slice is about 20 milliseconds long, and a slice like this is called a **frame**. >> The cuts are made in time, one after another, and they do not wait for a word to end. || **The slices go through a speech encoder.** The speech encoder is a network that was trained on lots of audio, and out comes one vector per slice. >> Different slices hold different sounds, so their vectors hold different numbers. || **So one second of speech becomes 50 vectors in time order.** That is a row, just like a sentence. >> Once speech is a row of vectors, the LLM can read it the same way it reads words.
12|**We take an image of an egg in a pan and cut it into a grid of small squares.** The squares are called **patches**, and this grid gives 16 of them. >> A patch is just one small piece of the image. || **We lay the patches out in a row, in reading order.** Now the image is a sequence, like a sentence, with a first patch and a last patch. >> The patches then go through a **vision encoder**, and out comes one vector per patch. || **Our grid is 4 by 4 only to keep it simple.** Real systems use tiny patches of 14 to 16 pixels, so one image gives hundreds of vectors. >> More patches means more vectors in the row for the same image.
13|**Now we put everything in one row.** First come the image patches, then the speech slices, then the words, with the last word hidden. >> The words are “Now,” “crack” and “the,” and the slot after them is empty. || **The old clues are still there.** The earlier words help the model guess the hidden word, exactly as before. >> The new thing is that a patch containing an egg, and the sound of cracking, also help. These are **cross-modal dependencies**, clues that cross from one sense to another. || **The game is exactly the same as before.** The model guesses the hidden piece from the other pieces. >> Some of the clues now come from other senses, and that is the only change.
14|**One example is used for the rest of the training story, and it is three cute puppies.** First there is an image of three puppies, cut into patches as before, 3 rows of 6, so 18 patches. >> Each patch is one piece of the image, just as with the egg. || **Then there is the same phrase, spoken, displayed as a spectrogram.** Time runs left to right, higher up means higher frequency, and darker means more energy. >> Each word sits in its own stretch of time, and each thin column of the spectrogram is a frame. The speech was made with a speech synthesizer, an MBROLA diphone voice. || **And there are the words themselves, as text.** These are three views of one event. >> In training, all three go into one row, and the model plays the hide-and-guess game on that row.
15|**This is the same game as before, played on a row with three senses.** First comes the image, then the sound, then the words, and the last word is hidden. >> The words the model can read are “three” and “cute.” || **Suppose the model only had the words “three cute.”** Many endings fit, such as “kittens,” “puppies” and “babies,” so its bets are spread out. >> Now add the clues from the other senses. The image contains puppies, and the sound contains the spoken word “puppies,” so the bet piles up on “puppies.” || **Then the true word is revealed, and the model is graded on how much it bet on the truth.** The truth is “puppies,” and the model bet 0.85 on it. >> It bet a lot on the truth, so the error is small, and the weights move only a little.
16|**Now the hidden piece is a piece of sound.** The row is reordered so that the image comes first, then the words, then the sound, which is like teaching the model to speak a sentence it was given. >> The sound is predicted frame by frame, left to right, and the frames to the right are not heard yet. || **The first clue comes from the earlier frames.** The speaker’s voice and the formants change smoothly, so the last few frames say a lot about the next one. >> The words say which sound should come next, and here it is the vowel in “pup.” The image contains puppies, so it backs up the words, but it is a weaker clue here. || **A frame is a column of numbers, one for how much energy there is in each frequency band.** The model’s guess is close to the truth but not exact, and the error is how far apart each number is. >> That error is what nudges the weights. Some systems predict a code for each frame instead of the raw numbers.
17|**Last, the hidden piece is a piece of the image.** The row is reordered again so that the words come first, then the sound, then the image, which is like teaching the model to draw what it was told. >> The patches are predicted in reading order, and the patches that are not drawn yet are faded. || **Earlier patches give the first clue.** Above is background, and to the left are two puppies, so the model has seen what a puppy here looks like. >> The words help in a different way. “Three” says a third puppy must appear, because only two have been drawn, and “puppies” says to expect dog ears. The sound carries the same words, so it is a backup for the text. || **The guess is a blurry puppy ear in the average colour of the first two puppies, and the truth is a dark puppy.** So the biggest error is the colour, because nothing in the words, the sound or the earlier patches said the third puppy would be dark. >> That error nudges the weights. Only systems that also produce images are trained this way, and systems that only read images usually skip this step.
18|**Every piece can be the hidden one, whether it is a word, a frame or a patch.** For each kind of hidden piece, the words give a clue, the sound gives a clue and the image gives a clue. >> For a hidden word, “three cute” wants a plural noun, the word was just spoken, and the image contains puppies. || **For a hidden frame, the words say which sound comes next, and the voice and formants carry on.** The image backs up which word is being said. >> For a hidden patch, “three” says one more puppy is coming, the sound gives the same words in heard form, and the fur and background carry on. || **Clues from the same sense mostly say how the signal continues.** Grammar does that for words, smooth formants do it for sound, and fur and background do it for the image. >> Clues from the other senses mostly say what the thing is, which is puppies, three of them. That is why training on all three senses together helps, because each sense fills gaps the others leave.
19|**So far the model only reads images and sound, and the remaining part is how it speaks.** Sound is compressed into discrete codes, and each code is like a new word added to the vocabulary. >> The model predicts the next code exactly as it predicts the next word, so it is the same game again. || **A decoder changes the codes back into a sound wave.** The model produces the codes, and the decoder produces the sound that we hear. >> Speaking therefore needs two parts, the prediction of codes and the decoding of those codes. || **Three things are new in all of this.** They are a shared currency of vectors, data where the senses appear together, and a schedule that protects what was learned. >> The learning rule is unchanged, and it is to find the dependencies in the signal. The signal just got wider.
