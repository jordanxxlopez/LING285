9|**An LLM learns by playing a guessing game with a hidden word.** In “The chef cracks the,” the model has read four words and the fifth is covered. Its task is to say what the fifth word is. >> Every earlier word helps. A chef who cracks something is probably cracking food, so the earlier words are clues, and these clue relationships are called **interdependencies**. >> The model cannot be sure, so it divides its bet. Most of the bet goes on “egg,” less goes on “nut,” and the least goes on “code.” || **The weights are then nudged so the next guess is better.** Once the true word is revealed, each weight changes by a small amount in the direction that gives the right word a bigger bet. >> The same game is played when the signal holds **words, sounds and images**. The hidden piece and the clues can then be any of the three.
10|**Text, speech and images arrive in three different forms.** Text is already a row of pieces, such as “Now,” “crack,” “the” and “egg.” Each piece can become one vector, so text is ready for the model almost as it is. >> Speech is **air pressure changing in time**. The pressure rises and falls without a break, and nothing in it is a word. >> An image is **a grid of light**. It has no words either, and its parts are spread over a surface instead of standing in a row. || **All three have to become rows of vectors.** A row of vectors is the only thing an LLM can read, so speech and images need a conversion before the model can use them. >> After the conversion, one model can read text, speech and images together.
11|**Speech becomes a row by cutting it into short slices of time.** Each slice is called a **frame** and lasts about 20 milliseconds. >> The slices are passed through a **speech encoder**, a neural network that was trained on a large amount of audio. For every slice that goes in, one vector comes out. >> Slices with different sounds give vectors with different numbers. || **One second of speech becomes 50 vectors in time order.** Fifty slices of about 20 milliseconds fill one second, and each slice gives one vector. >> The speech is now a row, just like a sentence. The model reads frame vectors one after another, the way it reads word vectors.
12|**An image becomes a row by cutting it into patches.** A **patch** is a small square of the image. A grid cuts the image of an egg in a pan into 16 patches. >> The patches are then laid out in **reading order**, which means left to right along the top and then left to right along each line below. The image has become a sequence, like a sentence. >> The patches pass through a **vision encoder**, and one vector comes out for each patch. || **Real systems cut the image much more finely.** The example uses a 4 by 4 grid to stay simple. Real systems use patches of 14 to 16 pixels, so one image gives hundreds of vectors. >> More patches means a longer row for the model to read.
13|**The three kinds of piece now sit in one row.** The image patches come first, the speech frames come second and the words come last. The words are “Now,” “crack” and “the,” and then comes a hidden word. >> The earlier words help as they always did. “Now crack the” already narrows down what the hidden word can be. >> Help now also arrives from the other senses. A patch containing an egg points to the hidden word, and so does the sound of cracking. A clue that crosses from one kind of signal to another is a **cross-modal dependency**. || **The game has not changed.** The model still guesses a hidden piece from the pieces it can read. >> What has changed is that some of the clues come from other senses, so the model has more to go on.
14|**“Three cute puppies” is one event given in three forms.** The first form is an image of three puppies, cut into 18 patches arranged as 3 rows of 6. >> The second form is the phrase spoken aloud, displayed as a **spectrogram**. Time runs from left to right, a higher position means a higher frequency, and a darker area means more energy. >> Each word has its own stretch of time, with “three” first, “cute” second and “puppies” last. Each thin column is one frame. The voice is a speech synthesizer, an MBROLA diphone voice. >> The third form is the three written words. || **These are three views of one event.** In training, all three are placed in one row. >> The model then plays the same game on that row. One piece is hidden, and the model guesses it.
15|**The first training example hides a word.** The row holds the image, then the sound, then the words “three” and “cute,” and the word after “cute” is hidden. >> Suppose the model had only the words. “Three cute” could be followed by “kittens,” “puppies” or “babies,” so its bets would be spread out with no clear winner. >> Now the other senses are added. The image contains puppies, and the sound contains the spoken word “puppies.” The bet piles up on “puppies,” and the other two words are left with very little. || **The true word is revealed, and the model is graded on its bet.** The true word is “puppies,” and the model had bet 0.85 on it. >> A bet that large means the error is small. A small error moves the weights only a little.
16|**The second training example hides a frame of sound.** The row is reordered to hold the image, then the words, then the sound. This is like teaching the model to speak a sentence it was given. >> The sound is predicted one frame at a time from left to right. The frames after the hidden one have not been heard yet. >> Three clues help. The earlier frames help most, because the voice and the **formants** (frequency regions with strong energy) change smoothly from frame to frame. The words say the next sound is the vowel in “pup.” The image contains puppies, which backs up the words, although it is a weaker clue here. || **The guess is compared with the true frame number by number.** A frame is a column of numbers, one for the energy in each of 16 frequency bands. The guess is close but not exact. >> The error is how far each number is from the true one, and that error nudges the weights. Some systems predict a code for each frame instead of the raw numbers.
17|**The third training example hides a patch of the image.** The row is reordered again to hold the words, then the sound, then the image. This is like teaching the model to draw what it was told. >> The patches are predicted in reading order, so the patches after the hidden one are not drawn yet. >> The earlier patches give background above and two puppies to the left, so the model knows what a puppy in this image looks like. The word “three” says a third puppy must appear, and “puppies” says to expect dog ears. The sound repeats the same words as a backup. || **The guess has the right shape and the wrong colour.** The model produces a blurry puppy ear in the average colour of the first two puppies, but the third puppy is dark. >> The colour is the biggest error because no clue said the third puppy would be dark. That error nudges the weights. >> Only systems that also produce images are trained this way. Systems that only read images usually skip this step.
18|**Any piece of the row can be the hidden one, and every sense offers a clue.** When a word is hidden, “three cute” calls for a plural noun, the word was just spoken in the sound, and the image contains puppies. >> When a frame is hidden, the words say which sound comes next, the voice and formants carry on from the earlier frames, and the image backs up which word is being said. >> When a patch is hidden, “three” says one more puppy is coming, the sound gives the same words in heard form, and the fur and background carry on from the earlier patches. || **The clue from the same sense does a different job from the other two.** It says how the signal continues, as grammar, smooth formants, and fur and background do. >> The other senses say what the thing is, which here is puppies, three of them. >> Training on all three senses together helps because each sense fills gaps the others leave.
19|**The model speaks by predicting codes for sound.** Sound is first compressed into discrete codes, such as 417, 82 and 903. Each code works like a new word in the vocabulary. >> The model predicts the next code exactly as it predicts the next word, using the earlier codes as clues. >> A **decoder** then changes the row of codes back into a sound wave. || **Three things are new, and one thing is unchanged.** The new things are a shared currency in which everything becomes vectors in one row, data where the senses appear together, and a schedule that protects what was already learned. >> The unchanged thing is the learning rule. The model finds the dependencies in the signal, and the signal has simply become wider.
