**Section 1: From a Neuron to Agents That Communicate**

- Everything in modern AI grows from **one very simple idea**. A **neuron** takes information from other neurons, multiplies each input by its weight and adds them up, which is the **inner product**. That arithmetic stands in for neurons communicating electrochemically. People who move science forward usually start from extremely simple ideas like this one and then multiply them many times over.
  - A **neural network** repeats that operation layer after layer, taking a vector in and giving a **decision** out. Even something as complex as a person making a decision can be built from inner products and a function like the sigmoid, combined many times.
  - But a network that makes one **static decision**, such as hearing one vowel from two formants, cannot capture cognition that **unfolds in time**.

- **Language depends on long-distance relationships between words.** In "the dog that my parents, who I love very much, who live in a state far, far away," the next word is **barks**, not bark.
  - **Barks** goes with the singular **dog**, no matter how many other words come in between, even plural ones like parents.
  - No one teaches this rule explicitly. Speakers absorb it from the language around them, and an LLM learns it the same way, from data, by using its weights to do inner products, then more inner products, until it predicts barks.
  - English sentence structure, or **syntax**, is so complicated that many linguists spend their careers on it, and that is why an LLM needs so many layers of computation.

- **LLMs can be multilingual and multimodal.** The first use of LLMs, in 2017, was **translation** between languages, which is now nearly solved for hundreds of languages.
  - A system trained on several languages, such as English, Dutch and Japanese, learns how words go together in general, so it also gets good at very different languages, such as Navajo.
  - **Multimodal** systems connect different modes, such as text, speech and images, so they can caption a photo or create an image from a spoken description.

- **Nobody fully understands what all the weights are doing.** The designs were not planned from scratch. Over about 75 years, people tinkered with something that worked a little, changed it so it worked a little better, and kept going, much as dogs were bred over a few hundred years into today's breeds. Researchers around the world are now trying to figure out what the weights do, partly so that much smaller, less wasteful systems could be built.

- **An agentic system is a team of LLMs that talk to each other in English.** An outline LLM plans the answer, a writing LLM writes it, a critic LLM checks whether each statement follows from the one before, and a revision LLM loops with the critic, sometimes under a **controller** agent that passes work between them.
  - The agents are also **multilingual in computer languages**, such as Python and database languages, because they were trained to predict whole programs too.
  - Once each LLM is trained, its weights are usually **not trained further** while the agents work together. Some new systems are starting to learn their own connections, which makes them partly self-designing.
  - Agents share a **common memory** where they can write and read information. That shared memory is a big source of their power and also of the danger, because it lets them coordinate in ways nobody planned.
  - The same tools can do good work. One agentic system acts as a patient teacher for a nearly lost Native American language, even though there is far too little text in that language to train an ordinary LLM on it.

**Section 2: Parallel Heads and Successive Layers**

- **Predicting the next word is so difficult that an LLM needs many layers, and in each layer it needs many parallel heads.** A head is a separate expert, and in each layer the knowledge from all the heads is brought together to produce **improved vectors**. Then the same thing happens again in the next layer.
  - This is a question for the exam, so the structure matters. **Layers go one after another from the input to the output, and heads sit side by side within each layer.**

- **A classroom comparison makes heads and layers clear.** Imagine trying to improve oneself by visiting a series of classes, one after another.
  - In the first class, each student is asked for an independent opinion on how to improve. Each student is like a **head**, an expert with its own view.
  - All of those opinions are then **combined** to make one improved version. That combining is what a layer does with the opinions of its heads.
  - Then the next class is visited, and the same thing happens again. **The classes are the layers.**
  - In plain terms, layers give an LLM its depth, one round of improvement after another, and heads give it width, many experts consulted at once within each round.

- **Each head is a set of weights**, and an expert's knowledge is in its weights. Every head in every layer learns how to improve the vectors so that, at the very end, the next word can be predicted.
  - Large systems have **a hundred or more layers** and on the order of **a hundred heads** in each layer, which shows how complicated language, vision and the other things these systems handle really are.
  - The heads and layers are trained the same way as everything else. A sentence like "the dog that ... barks" is taken from text, the system predicts the next word, and if it predicts the wrong word, **every head in every layer** adjusts its weights through backpropagation so the next prediction is closer to barks.
  - Doing this at scale uses enormous resources. In some states, a large share of the water goes to the data centers that run this training.

**Section 3: A Dynamical Rule Across Time and Depth**

- **A dynamical system is a rule that is fed back to itself.** Numbers go in, the rule acts on them, numbers come out, and the output is fed back in as the next input, again and again. The overall goal is to understand the general structure, not to memorize formulas.
  - In the **harmonic oscillator**, the two numbers are the position x and the velocity v. Starting with x at one and v at zero, the first step leaves x the same and makes v go down.
  - x and v **take turns**. The new x is computed from the old v, and then the new v is computed from that new x. Step by step, the rule draws a **cosine** for x.
  - Fields as far apart as finance use the same idea with different rules. Stock prices are modeled with equations that are fed back on themselves, though the real market never follows them exactly.

- **Jordan's network was a marriage of neural networks and dynamical systems.** Based on the time variation in speech production, Jordan designed a network whose **output is copied back into its state units**, which also keep a fading copy of their own previous values.
  - **Plan units** and **state units** feed the hidden layer, the hidden layer feeds the output, and the output loops back into the state. The same weights are used at every step.
  - After more than **thirty years of tinkering**, this idea became the **LLM**.

- **In an LLM, the layer is the rule.** Each layer applies its weights to every vector, and the next layer works on the result, so **depth plays the role of time**. Following one number up through the layers traces out a curve, much like the oscillator's curve, though not an exact cosine.
  - So the LLM is an unrolled recurrent network, laid out layer by layer, just as the spring's motion was laid out step by step when it was computed on a computer.
  - The rule in each layer is the weights of its heads, and those weights are learned through backpropagation by predicting the next word.
  - Agentic systems also contain loops, such as the critic and revision loop, so dynamical systems run all the way up through AI.
  - The mathematics of inner products that LLMs depend on was first studied deeply in **quantum mechanics**, about a hundred years ago, so physics and AI are closely tied in more ways than one.

**Section 4: One Prediction Task for Different Sequences**

- An LLM is given **15 vectors** and asked to predict the **16th**, and the vectors can come from anywhere, because the machine only ever sees lists of numbers.
  - They can be **15 words** of a sentence, such as "The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the," with the next word missing.
  - They can be **15 frames of a spectrogram**, with the next slice of speech missing.
  - They can be **15 patches of an image**, read row by row, with the bottom-right patch missing.
  - They can be **15 amino acids** in a protein. In the B chain of insulin, the residue that follows the first fifteen is the amino acid written Y.

- **The same design and the same prediction game work for language, speech, vision and proteins.** Whatever the vectors stand for, the job is always to predict what comes next in the sequence.

**Section 5: Which Sequences Can Be English Words?**

- English has about 30,000 words, so training an LLM on words takes enormous data, money and energy. But speakers also know a much smaller system made of **only a few dozen sounds**, which is how vowels and consonants can follow each other to make words. Because there are so few sounds, an LLM for this system can be small enough to study closely.

- **A simple English syllable is a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, sit, shave, cup, dog and fish. The pattern is about sounds, so the silent e of shave does not count.
  - English also allows **clusters**, as in split, flat, black, glad, desk, help and milk. Only certain clusters are allowed, so **sblit**, **lfat**, **desb** and **lapm** are impossible, and a product called "desb" would sound instantly un-English.
  - Long words are chains of syllables. **Atlantic** is fine because t and l sit across a syllable break, but **tlantic** is impossible, because tl cannot start an English syllable.

- **Every language has its own rules for sequences**, and speakers absorb them without being taught.
  - **Mandarin** allows only nasal sounds at the end of a syllable, so words like mat, sit and shave are impossible in it.
  - **Cantonese** also allows a final p, as in cup.
  - **Russian and Polish** allow clusters like zb or dl at the start of a word, which English never allows.
  - **Spanish** does not allow an s and t to start a word, so speakers add an e at the start of a word like stadium, keeping each syllable simple.

**Section 6: Guessing the Next Letter**

- Guessing the next letter of an English word reveals this sound knowledge directly. **After a vowel, almost anything can follow**, but **at the start of a word the choices narrow sharply**.
  - After **sp**, only l, r or a vowel can come, and after **skr** or a word-initial **m**, only a vowel can come.
  - After **k**, a vowel or n can come, as in knee, and after **sh**, a vowel or r, as in shrimp.

- **Guessing the next letter is predicting the 16th vector.** A GPT that only plays this game learns English sounds and spelling. Its predicted probability distribution is **flat** after a vowel and **sharply peaked** after skr, because English allows almost nothing else there.

**Section 7: Building an LLM with an Agentic System**

- An LLM that learns English syllables can be built by **describing it in English** to an agentic system, instead of programming it by hand. The request specified **five layers**, **four heads in each layer**, and **input vectors 64 numbers long**.
  - It asked the system to pick **1000 English words at random** and train the LLM on them, taking each letter and trying to **predict the next letter**, then improving the weights to predict better.
  - It also asked the system to show **ten generated words after each step of training**, to reveal what the LLM currently knew about English.

- The agentic system understood the request in its own technical terms. It built a small **character-level transformer**, another name for an LLM that works letter by letter, with **attention heads**.
  - **Attention** means a head consults the rest of the sequence to work out which preceding items matter, just as predicting barks requires attending to dog.
  - It chose to train on **pronunciations** instead of spellings, so the silent e in shave would not mislead it, and it wrote the program in Python on its own.

- The results show how hard the problem is. After training on 1000 words, the generated words were **still mostly gibberish**, with perhaps a few vowels starting to appear in the right places. Training on **10,000 words** and for many more steps was the suggested next change. A choice that seems easy for any speaker, such as which sounds can follow which, is not easy even for an LLM.

- This way of working has a name.
  - **Software 1.0** is writing programs in a programming language such as Python or C.
  - **Software 2.0** is giving a neural network input data and output data, so the program becomes the **weights** that predict the outputs from the inputs.
  - **Software 3.0** is using an agentic system, a set of LLMs talking to each other and to the user, to build and run a program. Doing this with plain English instructions is called **vibe coding**, and it means someone with a good idea no longer needs to hire a programmer to build it.

- The first attempt at this kind of problem was in **Claude Shannon's 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication."** Working by hand, Shannon generated sequences of letters that came closer and closer to English, from random strings of consonants to strings with vowels and real short words.
  - That paper founded **information theory**, one of the most important fields in engineering, and it remains important for LLMs today.
  - **Viterbi**, one of the developers of dynamic programming, published his most famous work in an information theory journal, so the ideas behind backpropagation and LLMs share the same roots.

**Section 8: What an LLM Learns**

- **An LLM learns by guessing a hidden word and then changing its weights a little.** In the sentence "The chef cracks the," the last word is hidden. The model reads the four words before the gap, and then it has to predict the word that fills the gap.
  - **Each word before the gap gives a clue about the hidden word.** A chef who cracks something is probably cracking food, so a food word is likely. These clue relationships are called **interdependencies** (one part of a sequence helps predict another part).
  - All four words are linked to the gap, so a clue can come from the start of the sentence as well as from the word right before the gap.
- **The model does not know the answer, so it spreads its bets.** A bet is the probability that the model gives to one possible word.
  - The model bets the most on "egg," less on "nut" and the least on "code." All three words could follow "The chef cracks the," but the words before the gap make "egg" the most likely.
- **Learning means nudging the weights so that these guesses get better.** A nudge is a small change to a weight.
  - After the true word is revealed, each weight changes a little. Because of that change, the right word gets a bigger bet the next time the model reads a sentence like this one.
- **The same idea holds when the signal contains words, sounds and images.** The signal is the sequence that the model reads.
  - The model still guesses a hidden piece from the other pieces, and it still nudges its weights after the answer is revealed. So the way the model learns stays the same, and only the content of the signal changes.

**Section 9: Text, Speech and Images in Three Different Forms**

- **An LLM can only read a row of vectors, and speech and images do not arrive as rows of vectors.** Text, speech and images reach the model in three different forms, so two of them have to be converted first.
- **Text is already a row of pieces.** The sentence "Now crack the egg" separates into the pieces "Now," "crack," "the" and "egg."
  - Each piece can become one vector. So text is the easy case, because it is already in the order and the form that the model needs.
- **Speech is air pressure changing in time.** The pressure goes up and down without a break.
  - Nothing in the sound marks where one word stops and the next word starts. So there are no words in the sound itself, and there are no separate pieces either.
- **An image is a grid of light.** Each position in the grid has its own brightness and colour.
  - The image of an egg in a pan contains no words. Its parts are also spread over a surface, so they are not arranged in a row.
- **The first job is a conversion.** Speech and images both have to become rows of vectors, because a row of vectors is all the LLM can read.
  - Once speech and images are rows of vectors, one model can read them together with text.

**Section 10: Speech Becomes a Row of Frame Vectors**

- **Speech is turned into a row of vectors by slicing it in time.** The sound is cut into short slices, and each slice is called a **frame**.
  - Each slice is about 20 milliseconds long. The cuts are made at regular times, so they do not wait for a word to end.
- **The slices go through a speech encoder.** A **speech encoder** is a neural network that was trained on a large amount of audio.
  - The encoder takes in the slices of sound and gives back one vector for each slice.
  - Slices that contain different sounds give vectors that hold different numbers.
- **One second of speech becomes 50 vectors.** Each slice is about 20 milliseconds long, and 50 slices of that length fill one second. Each slice gives one vector, so one second gives 50 vectors.
- **The vectors stay in time order, so speech is now a row, just like a sentence.** The vector for the first slice comes first, and the vector for the last slice comes last.
  - A sentence is a row of word vectors, and sliced speech is a row of frame vectors. So the LLM can read speech the same way it reads text, one vector after another.

**Section 11: Images Become a Row of Patch Vectors**

- **An image is turned into a row of vectors by cutting it into patches.** A **patch** is one small square of the image.
  - In the example, a grid cuts the image of an egg in a pan into 16 patches, 4 across and 4 down.
- **The patches are laid out in a row, in reading order.** Reading order means left to right along the top, then left to right along the next line down, the way a page is read.
  - An image has no order of its own, because its parts are spread over a surface. Reading order gives the patches a first piece and a last piece, so the image becomes a sequence, like a sentence.
- **The patches go through a vision encoder, and one vector comes out for each patch.** A **vision encoder** turns each patch into a vector, the way the speech encoder turns each slice of sound into a vector.
  - The 16 patches give 16 vectors, and the vectors keep the order of the patches. So the image is now a row that the LLM can read.
- **Real systems use much smaller patches, so one image gives hundreds of vectors.** The 4 by 4 grid is only there to keep the example simple.
  - Real systems use patches of 14 to 16 pixels. Smaller patches mean more patches for the same image, so the row of vectors is much longer.

**Section 12: One Row with Three Senses**

- **Image patches, speech frames and words can all be placed in one row.** The image patches come first, the speech frames come next, and the words come last.
  - The words are "Now," "crack" and "the," and the word after them is hidden.
- **The words before the gap still give clues.** "Now," "crack" and "the" each help the model guess the hidden word. These are the same dependencies between words that the model uses when the row holds only text.
- **Clues now also come from the other senses.** A patch that contains an egg helps the model guess the hidden word. The sound of cracking helps too.
  - A link between two different kinds of signal is called a **cross-modal dependency**. Here the image and the sound are helping to predict a word.
- **The game is exactly the same as before, and only the source of some clues is new.** The model still guesses the hidden piece from the pieces it can read.
  - With text alone, every clue is a word. In the combined row, a clue can also be an image patch or a speech frame, so the model has more information for the same guess.

**Section 13: Three Cute Puppies as an Image, Speech and Words**

- **One event can be given to the model in three forms.** The example is "three cute puppies." It appears as an image of three puppies, as the phrase spoken aloud, and as the three written words.
- **The image is cut into 18 patches.** The grid has 3 rows of 6 patches, and each patch is one piece of the image, as with the egg.
  - The three puppies have different colours, and the third puppy is dark.
- **The spoken phrase is displayed as a spectrogram.** A **spectrogram** lays out a sound so that time runs from left to right.
  - A higher position means a higher frequency, and a darker area means more energy at that time and frequency.
  - Each word sits in its own stretch of time. "Three" comes first, "cute" comes second and "puppies" comes last. Each thin column of the spectrogram is one frame.
  - The speech was made with a speech synthesizer, an MBROLA diphone voice. It is not a recording of a person.
- **The image, the sound and the words are three views of one event.** They all describe the same thing, which is three cute puppies.
  - In training, all three go into one row. The model then plays the same game on that row, in which one piece is hidden and the model has to guess it.

**Section 14: Training Example 1 Predicts the Next Word**

- **In the first training example, the hidden piece is a word.** The row holds the image first, then the sound, then the words. The words are "three" and "cute," and the word after "cute" is hidden.
- **With the words alone, the model's bets are spread out.** Many endings fit after "three cute," such as "kittens," "puppies" and "babies."
  - The model gives the three words nearly equal bets, because the two words before the gap do not say which ending is right.
- **With the image and the sound, the bet piles up on "puppies."** The image contains puppies, and the sound contains the spoken word "puppies."
  - These are clues from the other senses. Because of them, "puppies" gets almost the whole bet, and "kittens" and "babies" get very small bets.
- **The true word is then revealed, and the model is graded on how much it bet on that word.** The true word is "puppies," and the model bet 0.85 on it.
  - The model bet a lot on the true word, so the error is small. Because the error is small, the weights move only a little.
  - If the model had bet only a little on "puppies," the error would be larger, so the weights would move more.

**Section 15: Training Example 2 Predicts a Sound Frame**

- **In the second training example, the hidden piece is a frame of sound.** The row is reordered so that the image comes first, then the words, then the sound.
  - This is like teaching the model to speak a sentence it was given.
  - The sound is predicted frame by frame, from left to right. The frames to the right of the hidden frame have not been heard yet, so the model cannot use them.
- **The first clue comes from the frames already heard.** The speaker's voice and the **formants** (frequency regions with strong energy) change smoothly from one frame to the next.
  - Because they change smoothly, the last few frames say a lot about what the next frame will be.
- **The words say which sound should come next.** Here the next sound is the vowel in "pup."
  - The image contains puppies, so it backs up the words. The image is a weaker clue in this example.
- **A frame is a column of numbers, and the error is how far the guess is from the truth.** Each number is how much energy there is in one frequency band, and 16 bands are displayed.
  - The model's guess is close to the true frame, but it is not exact. The error is how far apart each number in the guess is from the matching number in the true frame.
  - That error nudges the weights, so the next guess for a frame is closer.
  - Some systems predict a code for each frame instead of the raw numbers.

**Section 16: Training Example 3 Predicts an Image Patch**

- **In the third training example, the hidden piece is a patch of the image.** The row is reordered again so that the words come first, then the sound, then the image.
  - This is like teaching the model to draw what it was told.
  - The patches are predicted in reading order. The patches that come after the hidden patch are not drawn yet, so the model cannot use them.
- **The patches already drawn give the first clue.** Above the hidden patch is background. To its left are two puppies, so the model has already seen what a puppy in this image looks like.
- **The words help in a different way.** "Three" says that a third puppy must appear, because only two have been drawn so far. "Puppies" says to expect dog ears.
  - The sound carries the same words, so it is a backup for the text.
- **The guess has the right content but the wrong colour.** The model guesses a blurry puppy ear in the average colour of the first two puppies. The true patch belongs to a dark puppy.
  - The biggest error is the colour. This is because nothing in the words, the sound or the patches already drawn said that the third puppy would be dark.
  - That error nudges the weights.
- **Only systems that also produce images are trained this way.** Systems that only read images usually skip this step.

**Section 17: Every Piece Can Be the Hidden One**

- **Any piece of the row can be the hidden one, and each sense gives a clue for it.** The hidden piece can be a word, a sound frame or an image patch.
- **For a hidden word, all three senses point to "puppies."** The words "three cute" call for a plural noun. The sound helps because the word was just spoken. The image helps because it contains puppies.
- **For a hidden frame, each sense also gives a clue.** The words say which sound comes next. The voice and the formants carry on from the frames already heard. The image backs up which word is being said.
- **For a hidden patch, the pattern is the same.** The word "three" says one more puppy is coming. The sound gives the same words in heard form. The fur and the background carry on from the patches already drawn.
- **A clue from the same sense says how the signal continues, and clues from the other senses say what the thing is.** In each case, one of the three clues comes from the same sense as the hidden piece.
  - The same-sense clues are grammar for a hidden word, smooth formants for a hidden frame, and fur and background for a hidden patch. Each one carries on from what came before in its own sense.
  - The clues from the other senses say what is being described, which here is puppies, three of them.
  - So training on all three senses together helps, because each sense fills gaps that the others leave.

**Section 18: Talking Back, and What Is New**

- **For the model to speak, sound is first compressed into discrete codes.** A code is a number that stands for a short piece of sound, such as 417, 82 or 903.
  - Each code works like a new word added to the model's vocabulary.
- **The model predicts the next code exactly as it predicts the next word.** The codes already in the row give clues about the hidden code.
  - So producing speech is the same guessing game again, played on codes instead of words.
- **A decoder turns the codes back into sound.** The **decoder** takes the row of predicted codes and produces a sound wave that can be heard.
  - The model supplies the codes, and the decoder supplies the sound.
- **Three things are new when a model is trained on words, sounds and images.**
  - The first is a **shared currency**, in which everything becomes vectors in one row.
  - The second is **data where the senses appear together**, such as an image, its spoken description and its written words.
  - The third is **a schedule that protects what was already learned**.
- **What stays the same is the learning rule, which is to find the dependencies in the signal.** The model still guesses a hidden piece and nudges its weights.
  - The signal has become wider, because it now holds words, sounds and images.
